Use this guide to record work and feedback that actually happened. Keep your own dates, contributions, problems, revisions, and evidence. Do not treat the plan's estimated hours or this generated build as evidence that a team member completed an activity.

## Player feedback

Ask permission before recording or collecting feedback. Collect only information needed for the project, and let people skip questions. Suitable prompts include:

1. Without help, could you move, find a resident, and begin a conversation? Where did you get stuck?
2. What did the village's three community values mean to you?
3. Which resource decision was difficult, and whose needs did you consider?
4. Did a resident's response help you understand the consequence of your decision?
5. Could you find a way forward after making a choice you regretted?
6. Was the quiet space comfortable to use or skip?
7. Which text, sound, visual, or control setting would you change?
8. What connection did you notice between the environment, fair access, and community well-being?

Report what players said and did, including counterexamples. Avoid claiming the game improved mental health or changed attitudes without a suitable research design.

## Reflection prompts

- **Self-awareness:** What part of the work interested you? What did you find difficult, and what evidence shows growth?
- **New challenges:** Which skill was new? Compare an early attempt with a later result.
- **Planning and commitment:** What did you plan, what changed, and how did you respond to a setback?
- **Collaboration:** What did you contribute personally? How did another person's feedback alter your work?
- **Global issues:** Which local or global examples informed your interpretation of pollution and unequal access? Cite the source and explain the limits of your comparison.
- **Ethical implications:** Does the game respect residents' agency? Does it imply that listening alone fixes structural problems? Which choices oversimplify real scarcity, and how could the next version improve them?

## Ethical implication review (plan task 5.2)

Work through these with the game open, ideally with a second person. Write down what you find, even when it is uncomfortable, and what you changed.

- **Agency.** In each conversation, does the supportive choice let the resident decide for themselves? Is the pressuring response a believable thing someone might say, and does the repair path avoid punishing the player forever?
- **No one is a diagnosis.** Check each resident's text. Does anyone become only their difficulty? Does Rowan keep their own voice?
- **Listening is not enough.** Does any line imply that a conversation alone fixes housing, work, flooding or pollution? The recap says so explicitly; check that the scenes do too.
- **Scarcity is not hidden.** Do the dilemmas show real costs on every option, including the one that looks best? Where does the game oversimplify real scarcity, and could a later version show more?
- **Who is missing.** For each decision in the recap, read "who it left out". Is it fair and specific? Who could still be missing that the game does not mention?
- **Tone.** Read the de-escalation scene aloud. Does either neighbour sound like a villain? Does any choice feel like a trick question?
- **Representation.** Which voices does the story include, and whose experience is simplified? Compare with an example from your own community and cite the source.
- **Outcome panels.** Do the groups named in the panels match the groups the text talks about? Would someone in one of those groups recognise themselves?

### Added with Hillside and Storm Season

- **The beds.** In the Hall, the player chooses between beds for elders, beds for families with children, or pooling the space. None is right, and each leaves someone with less. Read each result aloud. Does it make the person left with less sound like a failure, or like a real cost? Would someone in that group recognise themselves in the outcome panel?
- **Noor and the limits of peer care.** Noor says neighbours are not nurses, and the check-in rota has a column for when to call a professional. Check that no scene suggests a conversation or a rota can replace care. Check that the listening hour is described as listening, and not as treatment. If a real nurse or counsellor is available, ask them to read these scenes.
- **Kit.** Kit is a teenager whose work is dismissed in the first conversation if the player chooses badly. Does Kit read as a person with a view, or as a device for delivering river facts? Would a real teenager find the dialogue believable? Ask one.
- **Odile.** Odile is an elder who talks about the past and is not hurried. Does she have wants of her own beyond being listened to? Does any line treat her age as a problem to manage? The scene where she is left alone at night should read as a cost the village learns from, and not as a punishment of the player.
- **The storm as a disaster.** The storm has no victims, only costs. Is that honest, or does it make a disaster feel gentler than it is? Which real disasters has the team read about, and what did they show that the game leaves out?
- **Who was never met.** The Part Two recap says which neighbours the player never met and so could not help in advance. Is that fair to the player, and does it say something true about who gets looked after?
- **The hours.** Six hours and nine jobs make every plan leave something undone. Does the recap make that feel like a real trade-off, or like the player failed?

Record the date, who reviewed, the version of the game, and what changed afterwards. This is evidence of the process, not of the game's impact.
